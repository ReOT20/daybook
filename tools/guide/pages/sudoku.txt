# Sudoku
> One to nine, once per row, column and box

## How to play

Tap a square, then tap a digit. Tap the digit again to clear it.

Or pick the digit first: with no square selected, tap a digit, then every square it goes in. Tap the selected square to let go of it, and the digit again to finish.

Tap the pencil to take notes. While it is lit, a digit is pencilled small in the square instead of placed, and placing a digit clears it from the notes of the squares it sees.

On a keyboard, the arrows move, 1 to 9 place a digit (or a note in pencil mode), and Backspace clears.

Stuck? The first tap on Hint says where to look, and the second explains why. You make the move yourself, unless you ask for "Show me".

## Rules

- Fill every square with a digit from 1 to 9.
- No digit may repeat within a row, a column or a 3x3 box.
- The digits you start with are given, and stay put.
- Clashing digits are shown in red as you go.
- Every board has exactly one answer, and you never need to guess to find it.

## What changes per tier

- Standard: Each digit has one place left in a row, column or box. 34 or 35 digits are given.
- Hard: Some squares need every other digit ruled out. About 28 digits are given.
- Expert: Needs a pair, a locked digit or a what-if. About 28 digits are given.

## Techniques

These are the steps the hints teach, simplest first. Try them in this order. On each board, the tinted squares are where to look, rings mark the digits the reasoning leans on, and the filled square is the move. Small digits are pencil marks, and a struck one is a digit the step rules out. Every board here is a real position from the game, and this is the step the hint gives on it.

### full-house | The last square in a row, column or box

A row, column or box with one empty square is missing one digit, so that digit goes there. Look for these first: they need no searching at all.

~~~board
9 5 4 1 3 2 . 8 .
6 3 2 7 5 8 1 4 9
8 1 7 4 9 6 2 . .
1 2 3 6 4 9 5 . .
4 9 8 2 7 5 . . .
7 6 5 8 1 3 4 9 .
2 8 9 3 6 1 . . .
5 4 1 9 2 . 8 . .
3 7 6 5 8 . 9 2 .
box: 3
focus: r6c1-r6c9
answer: r6c9=2
caption: Row 6 has one empty square left, and 2 is the only digit it's missing.
~~~

### hidden-single-box | A digit's only place in a box

Pick a box and a digit it still needs. Every empty square in the box that already sees that digit, in its row, column or box, is ruled out. If only one square is left, the digit goes there.

~~~board
9 5 4 1 3 2 . 8 .
6 3 2 7 5 8 1 4 9
8 1 7 4 9 6 2 . .
1 2 3 6 4 9 5 . .
4 9 8 . 7 5 . . .
7 6 5 . 1 3 4 9 .
2 8 9 3 6 1 . . .
5 4 1 9 2 . 8 . .
3 7 6 5 8 . 9 2 .
box: 3
focus: r4c4-r6c6
cite: r5c3
answer: r6c4=8
caption: The centre box needs an 8. The upper empty square shares a row with the ringed 8, so the 8 goes in the lower one.
~~~

### hidden-single-line | A digit's only place in a row or column

The same idea along a line: every other empty square in the row or column already sees the digit, so there is only one place for it.

~~~board
5 . . . . 1 . 7 .
. . 6 4 . . . 5 .
8 . 7 5 9 3 2 . 6
. 2 8 9 5 . . 6 1
. 9 1 . . . 5 2 .
6 7 5 . 1 2 9 8 .
. 6 . 2 3 5 . . 8
. 8 . 7 . 9 6 . 5
. 5 . 1 . . . . 2
box: 3
focus: r6c1-r6c9
cite: r2c4
answer: r6c9=4
caption: Row 6 needs a 4. Its other empty square, in column 4, sees the ringed 4, so the 4 goes at the end of the row.
~~~

### naked-single | A square with one digit left

Now start from a square rather than a digit. Its row, column and box between them may hold every digit but one, and that one is all it can be. Hard's boards need these.

~~~board
. . . . . . . 6 2
6 . 4 3 2 5 . . 7
. 2 . . . 1 . 5 3
4 3 9 . 1 . . 2 .
. . 1 . 3 . 7 . .
. 5 . . 9 . 3 . 1
1 4 . 2 . . . 7 .
. . . . . 8 6 . 4
9 . . . . . 2 . .
box: 3
focus: r8c2
cite: r3c2 r4c2 r6c2 r7c1 r7c2 r8c6 r8c7 r8c9 r9c1
answer: r8c2=7
caption: The ringed digits are all this square can see. They hold every digit except 7.
~~~

### locked-candidates | A digit locked to one line or box

If all the places for a digit in a box lie in one row or column, the digit has to be in that part of the line, so the rest of the line can't have it. It works the other way too: if all a line's places for a digit lie in one box, the rest of that box can't have it. Either way, that can leave a square with one digit, or a digit with one place.

~~~board
. . 8 3 9 6 . 5 1
9 3 6 . . . 8 2 .
. . . . 2 8 6 . .
. 1 9 8 . . . . .
. . . . 7 . 1 . .
. . . . . 3 5 6 .
. 9 2 5 8 . . . 6
8 4 . . . . 9 1 5
5 6 . 4 . 9 2 . .
box: 3
focus: r1c1-r3c3 r3c4-r3c9
cite: r1c9
notes: r3c1=1 r3c3=1
answer: r3c4=7
caption: The ringed 1 keeps 1 out of the box's top row, so the box's 1 is in row 3. The rest of row 3 can't be 1, and that leaves this square only 7.
~~~

### naked-pair | Two squares sharing two digits

Two squares in a row, column or box that can only be the same two digits take both of them, even if you can't yet say which goes where. No other square there can be either digit.

~~~board
. 9 . . . 5 8 . .
7 . . . . . 9 . 5
8 . 5 9 7 . 3 2 6
9 . . 6 . . . 5 8
6 . 8 5 . 2 . 3 9
5 3 . . 9 8 . . 2
3 8 . . 2 6 5 9 .
. . 7 . . 9 . . 3
. . 9 1 . . . . .
box: 3
focus: r1c3-r9c3
notes: r6c3=14 r7c3=14
struck: r1c3=14 r2c3=14
answer: r4c3=2
caption: In column 3, two squares can only be 1 or 4, so they take both, and the squares above lose them. The square that could be 1, 2 or 4 is left with 2.
~~~

### hidden-pair | Two digits with only two places

The same pair, seen from the digits. If two digits fit only in the same two squares of a row, column or box, those squares hold them both and can't hold anything else.

~~~board
. . 3 . . . 1 9 2
. . 2 3 . . . 6 4
1 . 9 2 . . . 3 8
7 2 8 . 6 . 9 4 3
9 5 6 . . 3 2 1 7
4 3 1 . 2 . 6 8 5
2 1 4 6 5 8 3 7 9
. 9 5 . . 4 8 2 .
. . 7 . . 2 4 5 .
box: 3
focus: r1c4-r9c4
notes: r1c4=48 r5c4=48
struck: r1c4=57
answer: r4c4=5
caption: In column 4, 4 and 8 fit only in the top empty square and the one in row 5. So the top one can't be 5, and row 4's square is the only place left for 5.
~~~

### what-if | A what-if

Take a square with two digits left, or a digit with two places left, and suppose one of them. Follow the digits that forces, no more than a couple. If you reach a square or a line with nothing left, your supposition was wrong, so it's the other one. This isn't guessing: you only write down the digit once the other has been shown to break.

~~~board
4 3 7 9 8 5 6 2 1
5 . 2 6 3 . 4 . 9
9 . 6 . . . . . 3
2 . 8 . 1 6 . . 4
3 6 . 7 . 4 . 1 8
7 4 1 . 9 8 . . 6
8 7 . . 6 3 1 . 2
6 . 3 1 . 2 8 . 7
1 2 4 8 7 9 3 6 5
box: 3
focus: r4c4
cite: r5c3 r7c3 r7c4
notes: r5c3=5
struck: r7c3=5 r7c4=5
answer: r4c4=3
caption: This square is 3 or 5. If it were 5, the 5 of the box to its left would be forced into row 5, and then row 7 would have nowhere left for a 5. So it's 3.
~~~
